On the patience of well-made things

Why the objects that last are rarely the ones that hurry.

By M. Alcott — Issue 42

A chair that has been sat in for thirty years tells you something a new chair cannot. The wear is not damage; it is a record of use, written slowly into the wood.

We mistake speed for progress in most things. But typography, like furniture, rewards those willing to set a line and then look at it for a long time.

The best tools disappear into the work, leaving only the rhythm of making.

This is why the printed page, for all its supposed obsolescence, still teaches the screen how to behave: generous margins, a measure of sixty-odd characters, and silence where silence is due.

This issue was set in a single measure to preserve the reading rhythm across devices.
